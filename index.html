About

I am an Economics Ph.D. Candidate at the University of Michigan. I work on development and labor economics, with a focus on the role of religion, culture, and gender.

I am from Santiago, Chile. I graduated from Universidad de Chile with a B.A. and M.A. in Economics. I also obtained a Master of Public Policy from the University of Michigan in 2021.

I previously worked for the World Bank's Poverty Group and the Inter-American Development Bank.

The Price of Faith: Economic Costs and Religious Adaptation in Sub-Saharan Africa

with Eduardo Montero and Dean Yang

Revise and resubmit, The Quarterly Journal of Economics

How do economic costs affect religious choices, and how do religious institutions adapt to economic realities? We study the Seventh-day Adventist (SDA) church in Sub-Saharan Africa, which prohibits production of tobacco, coffee, and tea, creating opportunity costs for potential members in areas suitable for these crops. We construct a measure of SDA membership opportunity cost that varies over time and space based on local crop suitability and export prices. Using church administrative data and member surveys, we find that increased opportunity costs lead to lower membership growth and lower satisfaction with the church among existing members. Higher opportunity costs also cause establishment of new SDA educational and health institutions; church members to perceive less messaging about “healthy living” religious tenets; and higher church member consumption of prohibited goods. These findings provide new insights into the microeconomics of cultural change, as mediated by religious institutions. Religious organizations can show striking flexibility in balancing tradition and adaptation when faced with economic pressures.

The Roots of Faith: Early Life Droughts and Religious Commitment in Africa

June 2026

When in the life cycle does economic adversity turn people toward religion? I combine individual-level data from three rounds of the Afrobarometer survey with a global drought index to construct a life-cycle measure of drought exposure for more than 113,000 individuals in 452 localities across 34 African countries, and exploit variation in the timing of droughts across birth cohorts within localities. Drought exposure during the teenage years durably raises the probability that an adult belongs to and actively participates in a religious group, while exposure in childhood or adulthood leaves no lasting effect. The effect operates on participation rather than identity: teenage drought does not change whether individuals identify with a religion, only whether they take part. The result is robust to alternative age brackets, to a migration-robust design that assigns exposure by ethnic homeland rather than current residence, and to a cohort panel that recovers the same short-run response to contemporaneous droughts. Teenage exposure also raises long-run trust in community-based institutions, while exposure in adulthood shifts educational attainment, suggesting that religious participation is one strand of a broader adaptation to environmental shocks.

Seats Without Applicants: A Gender Quota in College Admissions

Draft in preparation

Affirmative-action reserves are designed for settings where qualified members of the target group apply and are rationed out. I study a gender quota whose binding constraint turns out to be the supply of applicants rather than of seats. Since 2024, Chile's "More Women Scientists" (Más Mujeres Científicas) policy has attached supernumerary women-only seats to hundreds of STEM programs in the national centralized admissions system. In the first year, 47.5% of the reserved seats were awarded, and 42% of covered programs awarded none, because no eligible woman had listed them. The missing applications trace to field choice rather than aspirations: conditional on stating university aspirations, girls reach STEM admission at 42% of the boys' rate. Recomputing the assignment without the reserves, the filled seats placed 596 additional women in STEM with no student made worse off, and a third of the downstream beneficiaries of the resulting vacancy chains are men. The female share of applications to covered programs rises 1.9 percentage points, with flat pre-trends over a decade, a response driven by the targeted announcement rather than by improved admission odds. Reallocating the existing seats toward programs where eligible women apply would raise the policy's output by roughly half.

The Transmission Technology: Japanese Migration to Peru and the Persistence of Ethnic Identity

Data collection and analysis in progress

Nearly all economic research on cultural transmission studies migration from poor to rich countries. This project inverts that setting. Between 1899 and 1923, Japanese contract laborers were assigned to Peruvian haciendas through a centralized brokerage, creating quasi-random variation in where they settled and in whether they arrived in districts already home to Chinese communities that had come two generations earlier. Linking hacienda placement records for more than 21,000 migrants to the 1876 and 1940 Peruvian censuses and to the full-count 2017 census, I ask whether traits such as savings and educational investment persist more strongly when the minority culture is economically advantaged, and whether a culturally proximate prior minority reinforces or dilutes that persistence.

Becoming a Teacher: Experimental Evidence from an Information Intervention

with Mariana Alfonso, Matías Busso, Hugo R. Ñopo, and Antonella Rivera

International Journal of Educational Development, Vol. 118, October 2025

Education systems seeking to improve outcomes must attract, develop, and retain highly effective teachers. A critical challenge is making the teaching profession appealing to talented youth. This paper presents evidence from an experiment in Peru, where we provided high school seniors with information about recent reforms to the teaching career. We find positive, though modest, effects on both the intensive and extensive margins: treated students were more likely to choose an education major and to enroll in higher education. While the intervention increased the number of prospective teachers, it did not substantially alter the profile of those entering the profession. These findings suggest that career incentives and information can influence students’ decisions, though broader changes to the composition of the future teaching workforce may require additional, complementary interventions.
